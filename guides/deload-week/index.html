Deload week: what it is and how to do it

By Jesse Sissala · Updated November 11, 2026 · 5 min read

A deload week is a planned week of easier training, usually with about half your normal sets at a lighter effort, that lets fatigue clear so you can keep progressing afterward.

Hard training builds fitness and fatigue at the same time. For a while, fitness wins and your lifts go up. Eventually fatigue piles up faster than it clears, and you feel flat, sore and stuck.

A deload is the planned pressure release. You keep training, but you train noticeably lighter for about a week. This guide explains when you need one, the two main ways to deload, how often to schedule them and what a deload week looks like in practice.

What a deload week is

In a 2023 Delphi consensus study, Bell and colleagues asked experienced strength and physique coaches to agree on how deloading should work. Their consensus definition describes a deload as a period of reduced training stress meant to reduce physical and mental fatigue, support recovery and prepare you for the next block of training.

Two things set a deload apart from simply taking time off:

  • You still train. You keep the same exercises and movement patterns, so your technique and the habit stay intact.
  • It is temporary and planned. A deload sits inside a training program, usually for about a week, and is followed by a return to normal or slightly harder training.

Deloads are common in practice but still lightly researched. Most of the advice below comes from coaching consensus and training principles rather than many controlled trials.

Signs you need a deload

One bad session means little. Look for several of these signs lasting a week or more:

  • Your main lifts have stalled or dropped for two or more sessions in a row.
  • Weights that normally feel moderate suddenly feel heavy, even after a good night's sleep.
  • Joints or tendons ache in a nagging, persistent way, not just normal muscle soreness.
  • Soreness from one session still lingers when the next one starts.
  • You dread workouts you usually enjoy, or your motivation has dropped.
  • Your sleep quality has worsened while training is at its hardest.
If you track effort with RPE or RIR, a useful warning sign is when the same weight and reps feel 1–2 RPE harder than they did a week or two earlier.

Sharp pain, swelling or pain that changes how you move is not a deload signal; it is a reason to stop that exercise. If you have an injury or a health condition, check with a doctor or physical therapist before training through it.

How to deload: volume vs intensity

There are two main levers. You can reduce volume (fewer sets and reps) or intensity (lighter weights and more reps in reserve). Most lifters combine them.

Common deload methods
MethodWhat you changeBest for
Volume deloadCut sets by about 40–50%, keep weights about the sameStrength athletes who want to keep the feel of heavy loads
Intensity deloadKeep sets, drop weight by about 10–20%Joint and tendon aches, technique work
Combined deloadCut sets by about half and stay 3–4 reps from failureGeneral lifters; the simplest default
Active restSwap lifting for walking, cycling or light mobilityMental burnout, travel weeks

Whichever you choose, the goal is the same: finish every session feeling like you could have done a lot more. If a deload workout leaves you tired, it was not a deload.

Sample deload week

Here is one lower-body session from a normal training week compared with the same session during a combined deload.

Lower-body day: normal week vs deload week
ExerciseNormal weekDeload week
Back squat4 × 6 at 100 kg, RPE 82 × 6 at 90 kg, RPE 6
Romanian deadlift3 × 8 at 80 kg, RPE 82 × 8 at 70 kg, RPE 6
Leg press3 × 12, RPE 8–92 × 10, RPE 6
Leg curl3 × 12, RPE 92 × 10, RPE 6
Calf raise4 × 12, RPE 92 × 12, RPE 6

Apply the same pattern to every session in the week: about half the sets, around 10% less weight and 3–4 reps in reserve. Keep your usual training days so the routine stays in place. The plate calculator is handy for loading the lighter weights quickly.

How often to deload

There is no single correct interval. A common approach is to plan a deload every 4–8 weeks of hard training and adjust based on how you respond. The ACSM recommends that intermediate and advanced lifters vary their training in a periodized way, and a scheduled easy week is one of the simplest forms of that.

Typical deload frequency
LifterTypical intervalNotes
Beginner (under 1 year)Often not needed on a scheduleDeload when fatigue signs appear
IntermediateEvery 6–8 weeksPlan it at the end of a training block
Advanced, high volumeEvery 4–6 weeksHeavier loads and more sets build more fatigue
Older lifters or high life stressEvery 4–6 weeksRecovery tends to be slower

Planned and reactive deloads both work. Planning makes them easier to stick to; reacting to fatigue signs avoids taking one you do not need. Many lifters do both: a planned deload with permission to take one early.

What the research shows

In a 2024 trial, Coleman and colleagues had trained lifters either train continuously for nine weeks or stop lifting entirely for one week in the middle. Muscle growth was similar in both groups, but the continuous group gained slightly more lower-body strength.

That study tested complete rest, not a reduced-training deload. A reasonable reading is that a week of easier training does not cost you muscle, and that keeping some training in the week, rather than stopping entirely, may be the safer choice if strength is your priority.

After the deload

Return to your normal sets and weights the following week. Many lifters feel stronger in the first session back. Use that to restart progressive overload, not to test a new max on day one.

If you are still flat after a deload, look outside the gym: sleep, stress and eating enough. Kaizado builds deload weeks into its multi-week plans, so the easier week is already scheduled when you reach it.

Frequently asked questions

Will I lose muscle during a deload week?

No. Research on a full week off found no difference in muscle growth, and a deload still includes training.

Should I take a full week off instead of deloading?

You can, but light training keeps your technique sharp and may preserve strength slightly better. Full rest is reasonable if you are sick, traveling or mentally burned out.

How long should a deload last?

About one week is the most common choice. Some lifters use 4–5 days; longer than two weeks starts to become a break rather than a deload.

Do beginners need deload weeks?

Usually not on a fixed schedule. Beginners lift relatively light loads and recover quickly, so they can deload only when fatigue signs appear.
