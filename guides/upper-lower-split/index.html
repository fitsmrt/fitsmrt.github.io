Upper lower split: the complete 4-day guide

By Jesse Sissala · Updated November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

An upper lower split divides your training into upper-body days and lower-body days, usually four sessions a week, so every major muscle gets trained twice weekly with enough recovery in between.

The upper lower split is one of the most practical ways to organize strength training once you can commit to four sessions a week. It is simple to schedule, hits each muscle group twice per week, and keeps sessions to a manageable 60 to 75 minutes.

This guide covers how the split works, who gets the most out of it, a ready-to-run 4-day schedule, four sample workouts, how to add weight over time, and the mistakes that most often stall progress.

What is an upper lower split?

On an upper lower split, each session trains either your upper body (chest, back, shoulders, arms) or your lower body (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves, and usually core). You alternate the two, typically running each one twice per week.

Most versions use two variations of each day, often called Upper A, Lower A, Upper B and Lower B. The A days lean toward heavier compound lifts in lower rep ranges, while the B days use different exercises and slightly higher reps. That variety spreads stress across joints and lets you train a wider range of movements without making any single session too long.

The main reason the split works is frequency. A 2016 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld and colleagues found that training a muscle at least twice per week produced more growth than once per week. A 2019 follow-up suggested that when total weekly volume is equal, frequency matters less, so the real advantage is practical: two sessions per muscle let you spread 10 or more weekly sets into workouts where you can still lift with good effort.

Who the upper lower split is for

The upper lower split fits a wide range of lifters, but it shines in a few situations:

  • Late beginners and intermediates who have run a full body routine for a few months and want more volume per session without longer workouts.
  • People with four training days available. Two on, one off, two on, two off fits most work weeks.
  • Lifters chasing both strength and size. Heavier A days and higher-rep B days cover both goals in one program.
  • Anyone who wants recovery built in. Your legs rest while you train upper body, and vice versa.

If you can only train two or three days a week, a full body plan will usually serve you better. If you want six days in the gym, push pull legs is often a better fit.

The 4-day upper lower schedule

The most common layout trains Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Friday. The rest day in the middle lets both halves of your body recover before the second round.

Sample upper lower workouts

Rest 2 to 3 minutes between sets of the big compound lifts and 60 to 90 seconds between sets of smaller isolation exercises. Aim to finish most sets with 1 to 3 reps left in the tank rather than grinding to failure.

Across the week this gives roughly 10 direct sets for chest, 14 for back, 13 for quads and 9 for hamstrings, plus indirect work from compound lifts. That sits within the range most research supports for growth; see how many sets per muscle if you want to fine-tune it.

How to progress on an upper lower split

The simplest reliable method is double progression: you work within a rep range and only add weight once you own the top of it.

  1. Pick a weight you can lift for the bottom of the range on every set with 1 to 3 reps in reserve.
  2. Each session, try to add a rep or two while keeping the weight the same.
  3. When you hit the top of the range on all sets with good form, add weight next session: about 5 lb (2.5 kg) for upper-body lifts and 5 to 10 lb (2.5 to 5 kg) for squats and deadlifts.
  4. The added weight will pull your reps back toward the bottom of the range. Build back up and repeat.

Use the one-rep max calculator to pick sensible starting weights, and the plate calculator to load the bar quickly. For a deeper look at adding load, reps and sets over time, read the progressive overload guide.

Every 6 to 8 weeks, or whenever performance drops for two sessions in a row, take a lighter week with about half the sets at the same or slightly reduced weights before building again.

Common upper lower split mistakes

  • Turning upper days into marathons. Upper body has more muscles to cover, so it is tempting to add exercise after exercise. Cap sessions at 6 to 8 exercises and let the second upper day pick up the rest.
  • Going light on lower days. Lower days are hard, and many lifters quietly drop sets. Keep hamstrings, glutes and calves in the plan, not just squats.
  • Making every day a max-effort day. A and B days should feel different. If every session is heavy, joints and recovery suffer.
  • Changing exercises every week. You need several weeks on the same lifts to see whether you are actually getting stronger. Keep your main lifts for at least one 6- to 8-week block.
  • Training to failure on compound lifts. Occasional failure on isolation work is fine, but failing heavy squats or bench presses regularly adds fatigue without adding much benefit.
  • Skipping the warm-up. A few ramp-up sets before your first heavy lift make the work sets safer and stronger.

Variations for different schedules

The split adapts well when four days does not fit every week:

  • 3 days a week: Rotate Upper A, Lower A, Upper B one week and Lower B, Upper A, Lower A the next. Each muscle gets trained about 1.5 times per week.
  • 5 days a week: Run the four standard days and add a fifth session for weak points, such as arms and shoulders, or an extra upper day.
  • Short on time: Drop the last isolation exercise from each day. The compound lifts deliver most of the results.

Frequently asked questions

Is an upper lower split good for building muscle?

Yes. It trains every major muscle twice per week and makes it easy to reach 10 or more weekly sets per muscle, which research associates with greater muscle growth than lower volumes.

Can beginners do an upper lower split?

Yes, as long as they can train four days a week. Many beginners do well starting with two or three full body sessions per week, then moving to an upper lower split after a few months.

Is upper lower better than push pull legs?

Neither is better overall. Upper lower suits four training days, while push pull legs suits three or six. On four days, upper lower gives each muscle more frequency than a 3-day PPL.

How long should an upper lower workout take?

Most sessions take 60 to 75 minutes including a warm-up. If yours run much longer, cut isolation exercises or shorten rest on smaller movements to 60 seconds.
